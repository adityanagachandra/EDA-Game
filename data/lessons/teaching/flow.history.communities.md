# EDA research conferences and communities

EDA research is published mainly at conferences, and each major conference has a distinct community and focus. Knowing them tells a tool designer where the state of the art for each engine is published, where public benchmarks and contests come from, and which papers a new idea must be compared against.

## The general conferences

The Design Automation Conference (DAC), held since 1964, is the largest EDA event, combining research papers with an industry exhibition. The International Conference on Computer-Aided Design (ICCAD) is a research-focused conference covering the whole field. Design, Automation and Test in Europe (DATE) is the main European venue, and the Asia and South Pacific Design Automation Conference (ASP-DAC) the main Asian one. The flagship journal is IEEE Transactions on Computer-Aided Design of Integrated Circuits and Systems (TCAD), alongside ACM Transactions on Design Automation of Electronic Systems (TODAES). Most influential EDA algorithms first appeared at one of these venues, so a literature search for any engine starts with their proceedings.

## The specialist communities

Several smaller venues anchor specific engines. The International Symposium on Physical Design (ISPD) focuses on partitioning, placement, routing and timing closure, and its annual contests released benchmarks that drove placement and routing research. The International Test Conference (ITC) is the main venue for testing and DFT. Computer Aided Verification (CAV) and Formal Methods in Computer-Aided Design (FMCAD) publish model checking, SAT and SMT techniques that formal tools adopt. The International Workshop on Logic and Synthesis (IWLS) covers synthesis. The FPGA symposium covers FPGA architecture and CAD. Circuit conferences such as ISSCC are where designers, not tool builders, publish.

## Benchmarks and contests

Progress in EDA is measured on shared benchmarks. Placement and routing research uses suites released with ISPD contests; logic synthesis uses the ISCAS and EPFL benchmark suites; formal verification uses the hardware model checking competition (HWMCC) and SAT competitions; test uses ISCAS circuits. Contests at ICCAD and ISPD set new problems each year with industrial-style data. A credible new algorithm is compared against the best published results on these benchmarks, with its run time, and increasingly with open-source code so others can reproduce it. Benchmarks also have limits: old suites may not reflect current design styles, so strong papers report results on several suites.
